Celsius, Fahrenheit and Kelvin: How to Convert Temperature

Quick answer

Why temperature conversion needs an offset as well as a factor, the formulas, and quick tricks for estimating in your head.

By 123MiniApps · Published 2026-08-01 · Updated 2026-09-01 · 1040 words · about 5 minute read

Temperature conversion is slightly different from converting lengths or weights, because temperature scales do not start from the same zero. Converting Celsius to Fahrenheit therefore needs both a multiplication and an addition, not just a single factor. The Temperature Converter handles Celsius, Fahrenheit and Kelvin instantly, and this article explains the formulas, why the offset exists, and some handy tricks for estimating temperatures in your head.

Whether you are following a recipe written in the other system, understanding a foreign weather forecast, or working through a science problem, knowing how the scales relate turns a confusing number into a meaningful one.

Why temperature needs an offset

Most conversions are pure multiplication because both units measure from the same starting point, zero centimetres and zero inches are the same length, namely nothing. Temperature scales are different: zero degrees Celsius (the freezing point of water) is not the same temperature as zero degrees Fahrenheit. The two scales are offset from each other, and their degrees are also different sizes. Converting between them therefore requires scaling for the different degree size and shifting for the different zero point. That is why the formula has both a multiply and an add.

The formulas

The three scales convert with these relationships:

  • Celsius to Fahrenheit: multiply by 9/5 (1.8), then add 32.
  • Fahrenheit to Celsius: subtract 32, then multiply by 5/9.
  • Celsius to Kelvin: add 273.15 (Kelvin uses the same degree size as Celsius, just a different zero).
  • Kelvin to Celsius: subtract 273.15.

The order matters in the Fahrenheit conversions: for F to C you must subtract 32 first and then scale, not the other way around, or you will get the wrong answer. This is the single most common temperature-conversion mistake.

The scales cross at -40

Celsius and Fahrenheit read the same at exactly -40 degrees: -40°C = -40°F. It is a neat check on the formula, and a reminder that the two scales genuinely diverge above and below that point.

Mental-maths shortcuts

You rarely need an exact answer to make sense of a temperature, and a couple of shortcuts get you close. To convert Celsius to Fahrenheit quickly, double the Celsius number and add 30, for 20°C that gives 70, against the exact 68, which is close enough to know it is mild. Going the other way, subtract 30 from the Fahrenheit and halve it. These approximations drift a little at extreme temperatures but are excellent for everyday weather. Learning them means you can interpret a foreign forecast at a glance without reaching for anything.

Which scale is used where

Context tells you which conversion you need. Most of the world uses Celsius for weather, cooking and body temperature. The United States uses Fahrenheit for the same everyday purposes, which is why recipes and forecasts often need converting. Kelvin is the scientific standard, used in physics and chemistry because it starts at absolute zero, the coldest possible temperature, which makes many physical laws simpler to express. Knowing the domain you are in tells you the destination scale before you even start.

Common situations where conversion matters

Temperature conversion turns up more often than you might expect: following an American recipe that lists oven temperatures in Fahrenheit while your oven is in Celsius, understanding a weather app set to the wrong scale, checking whether a fever reading in one scale is serious, or working a science problem that mixes Celsius data with Kelvin formulas. In each case the same formulas apply. For oven temperatures in particular, getting the conversion right matters, the difference between 180°C and 180°F is the difference between a properly baked dish and a raw one.

Where the three scales came from

The three temperature scales each reflect the priorities of their creators, which is why they sit at different zeros. Fahrenheit, devised in the early eighteenth century, set its zero at the coldest temperature its inventor could reliably reproduce with a brine mixture, and placed body temperature and water's freezing point at seemingly arbitrary numbers, which is exactly why it feels irregular today. Celsius came later with a cleaner idea: put zero at water's freezing point and one hundred at its boiling point, giving a scale neatly divided into a hundred degrees, ideal for everyday and scientific use alike.

Kelvin took the logic one step further for science. Rather than anchoring to water, it starts at absolute zero, the theoretical point at which molecular motion stops and nothing can get colder. Because it begins at the true bottom of the temperature range, Kelvin has no negative values, which makes it the natural scale for physics, where temperature appears in equations describing gases, radiation and energy. It shares Celsius's degree size, so converting between them is just adding or subtracting 273.15. Knowing this history explains the whole picture: Fahrenheit is historical and human, Celsius is decimal and practical, and Kelvin is absolute and scientific. Each survives because it suits its domain, and converting between them is simply a matter of respecting their different starting points and, for Fahrenheit, its different degree size too.

For everyday confidence, it helps to anchor a few reference points in both common scales: water freezes at 0°C (32°F) and boils at 100°C (212°F); a comfortable room is around 20°C (68°F); normal body temperature is about 37°C (98.6°F); and a hot summer day is around 30°C (86°F). Memorising even two or three of these gives you an instant feel for any temperature you meet, so you can tell at a glance whether a foreign forecast means reach for a coat or reach for sunscreen, with the converter there for the exact figure whenever a recipe or a calculation demands precision.

To wrap up: temperature is special among conversions because its scales have different zero points as well as different degree sizes, so you scale and shift rather than just scale. Remember to subtract 32 before scaling when going from Fahrenheit to Celsius, lean on the double-and-add-30 trick for quick estimates, and let the converter handle exact figures for recipes and science. Understand the offset and the three scales stop being confusing and become just another predictable conversion.

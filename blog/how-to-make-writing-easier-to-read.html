How to Make Your Writing Easier to Read (Readability Scores Explained)

Quick answer

What readability scores measure, why shorter sentences and plainer words win, and how to write for your actual audience.

By 123MiniApps · Published 2026-08-02 · Updated 2026-10-03 · 1131 words · about 5 minute read

Readability is how easy a piece of writing is to understand, and it can be measured. Readability scores turn features of your text, mainly sentence length and word length, into a number that estimates the reading level required to follow it. The Readability Checker calculates these scores in your browser and flags the sentences dragging your writing down, and this article explains what the scores mean and how to write more clearly.

Clear writing is not about dumbing down; it is about respecting the reader's time and attention. Even expert audiences prefer text they can absorb quickly, and complex ideas are best carried by simple sentences. Readability scores give you an objective check on whether your writing is as clear as you think it is.

What readability scores measure

The most common readability formulas, such as Flesch Reading Ease and Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level, are built from two simple ingredients: the average length of your sentences and the average length of your words (usually counted in syllables). Long sentences and long words push the difficulty up; short sentences and short words bring it down. Flesch Reading Ease produces a score from roughly 0 to 100 where higher is easier, a score in the 60s to 70s is comfortable for a general audience, while the Grade Level version estimates the school grade needed to read the text. They are approximations, not verdicts, but they correlate well with how hard text feels.

Why sentence length matters most

Of the two ingredients, sentence length is the one you can most easily control and the one that makes the biggest difference. A long sentence forces the reader to hold many ideas in mind at once before reaching the point, which is tiring and error-prone. Breaking one sprawling sentence into two or three short ones instantly lightens the load, even without changing a single word choice. The classic advice, vary your sentence length, but keep the average short, exists because a run of long sentences is where readers get lost. A readability checker that highlights your longest sentences is really pointing at the exact places to split.

Aim for your audience, not the lowest score

The goal is not the highest possible readability score but the right level for your readers. Technical documentation for engineers can sit higher than a consumer landing page. Know your audience, then use the score to check you are not accidentally harder than you meant to be.

Practical ways to improve readability

A handful of edits reliably make writing clearer:

  • Shorten long sentences: split them at natural break points, usually where a conjunction joins two complete ideas.
  • Prefer short, common words: 'use' over 'utilise', 'help' over 'facilitate'.
  • Cut filler: 'in order to' becomes 'to', 'due to the fact that' becomes 'because'.
  • Use active voice: it is shorter and clearer than the passive.
  • Break up walls of text: short paragraphs and the occasional list give the eye somewhere to rest.

None of these require special skill; they are mechanical improvements you can apply to any draft, and together they can move a piece from difficult to comfortable in a single editing pass.

Readability is not the whole story

It is worth keeping readability scores in perspective. They measure sentence and word length, not meaning, logic or accuracy, you can write perfectly readable nonsense, and a high score does not guarantee good writing. Very short, choppy sentences can also score well while reading unpleasantly, because rhythm and variety matter too. Treat the score as one useful signal among several: a prompt to look at your longest sentences and hardest words, not a target to game. The best writing usually has a comfortable score and reads naturally, because clarity and good rhythm tend to go together.

Reading, listening and length

Readability pairs well with the other checks that make writing land. Reading a draft aloud, or having a text-to-speech tool read it back, exposes clumsy, overlong sentences that the eye glides over, complementing what the score flags. Checking length with a word counter keeps a piece from overstaying its welcome, and a summariser can help you see whether your core point survives compression. Clear structure, comfortable sentences and the right length together are what make writing genuinely easy to read.

Why readability helps even expert readers

A persistent myth is that simpler writing is only for less capable readers, and that experts prefer dense, complex prose. Research into how people actually read points the other way: even highly educated specialists read clearer writing faster, understand it better, and rate its authors as more intelligent, not less. The reason is that reading takes cognitive effort regardless of expertise, and effort spent decoding a tangled sentence is effort not spent thinking about the idea it contains. When you make text easy to read, you free the reader's attention for your argument rather than your syntax.

This matters because the temptation to write impressively, long words, elaborate sentences, technical flourishes, is strong, especially when you want to be taken seriously. But the writers who are genuinely respected for their clarity, from scientists to essayists, tend to write plainly about complex things. Plain writing is harder to produce, not easier; it requires you to understand your subject well enough to express it simply. A readability score is a useful nudge against the impressive-but-impenetrable instinct, reminding you that the goal is to transfer an idea into someone else's head as efficiently as possible. Whether your audience is the general public or a room of specialists, clearer writing serves them better, and a quick readability check is a cheap way to keep yourself honest about whether you have written to communicate or merely to sound clever.

In summary, readability scores estimate how hard your writing is to read from sentence length and word length, and the single most effective improvement is usually to shorten long sentences. Prefer plain words, cut filler, favour active voice, and break up dense text. Aim for the level that suits your audience rather than the highest possible score, and remember that readability measures clarity of form, not quality of thought. Used as a prompt to edit rather than a target to chase, a readability checker is a simple, honest mirror for your writing.

Frequently asked questions

How do I make my writing easier to read?

Use short sentences and short paragraphs, prefer plain everyday words over jargon, put the main point first, break text with subheadings and white space, and read it aloud to catch anything clumsy. Small changes add up to much clearer writing.

What is a good reading level to aim for?

For a general audience, aim for roughly a grade 7 to 9 reading level, which most readability tools report. That is not dumbing down; even expert readers prefer clear, low-effort writing, especially on screens where people skim.

How long should sentences and paragraphs be?

Keep most sentences under about 20 words and vary their length so the rhythm does not feel flat. On the web, paragraphs of one to three sentences are easiest to scan, with plenty of white space between them.

Does readability affect SEO?

Indirectly, yes. Clear, well-structured writing keeps readers on the page longer and is easier for search and answer engines to understand and quote, which helps. Readability is not a direct ranking number, but it supports everything that is.

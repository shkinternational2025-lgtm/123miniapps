How Roman Numerals Work and How to Convert Them

Quick answer

The seven letters, the subtraction rule that trips everyone up, and where Roman numerals still appear today.

By 123MiniApps · Published 2026-07-28 · Updated 2026-09-01 · 1071 words · about 5 minute read

Roman numerals are the letter-based number system of ancient Rome, still used today on clock faces, in book chapters, on monuments and to number events like the Super Bowl. They are built from just seven letters, and reading them comes down to a couple of simple rules, plus one rule, subtraction, that catches nearly everyone out. Once you know the seven symbols and how they combine, you can read the year on a building or the copyright date at the end of a film, often the only place many people still meet Roman numerals in the wild.

The seven symbols

Every Roman numeral is built from seven letters, each with a fixed value:

  • I = 1
  • V = 5
  • X = 10
  • L = 50
  • C = 100
  • D = 500
  • M = 1,000

Numbers are formed by combining these letters, generally from largest to smallest, and adding their values. So MDC is 1,000 + 500 + 100 = 1,600, and XXVII is 10 + 10 + 5 + 1 + 1 = 27. For the most part, you simply read left to right and add, which is why the system feels intuitive until you hit the subtraction rule.

The subtraction rule that trips everyone up

To avoid writing four identical letters in a row, Roman numerals use subtraction: when a smaller-value letter appears immediately before a larger one, you subtract it rather than add. So IV is 5 − 1 = 4, not IIII, and IX is 10 − 1 = 9. The same applies at higher values: XL is 40, XC is 90, CD is 400, CM is 900. This is the rule that makes reading harder, because you cannot simply add every letter, you have to notice when a small letter sits before a bigger one and subtract instead.

  • IV = 4, IX = 9 (ones subtracted from fives and tens)
  • XL = 40, XC = 90 (tens subtracted from fifties and hundreds)
  • CD = 400, CM = 900 (hundreds subtracted from five-hundreds and thousands)
Only certain subtractions are allowed

You cannot subtract just any letter. Only I, X and C are used as subtractors, and only before the next one or two larger symbols. That is why 99 is XCIX (90 + 9), not the tempting but invalid IC. The rules are stricter than they first appear.

Why there is no zero

Roman numerals have no symbol for zero, and no concept of place value in the modern sense. This is not an oversight so much as a reflection of what the system was for, counting and recording quantities, rather than calculating. The absence of zero and place value is exactly why arithmetic is so awkward in Roman numerals and why the world eventually adopted the decimal system with its zero and positional notation. It is a useful reminder that a number system is a piece of technology, and some are far better suited to calculation than others.

Where Roman numerals still appear

Despite being ancient, Roman numerals persist in specific niches: clock and watch faces, the introductory page numbers of books, chapter and section headings, the names of monarchs and popes, movie copyright dates, annual events like the Olympics and championship games, and building cornerstones. In most of these, the numerals are chosen for their formal, traditional feel rather than any practical advantage. Being able to convert them is handy precisely because they turn up in these fixed, formal contexts where you need to read a specific number.

Reading a year on a monument

The most common real use is decoding a year, such as a film's copyright date or an inscription. The method is to break the numeral into its thousands, hundreds, tens and units and add them, watching for subtractions. MCMLXXXIV, for example, is M (1,000) + CM (900) + LXXX (80) + IV (4) = 1984. It looks daunting as one block but resolves easily once you chunk it. Common mistakes and modern conventions

Roman numerals invite a few predictable errors, most of them stemming from the subtraction rule. The classic mistake is over-subtracting: writing IC for 99 seems logical but is invalid, because only specific subtractive pairs are allowed, 99 must be written XCIX, as 90 plus 9. Another is repeating a symbol too many times; in the standard modern convention a letter is not repeated more than three times in a row, which is the whole reason subtraction exists (IV rather than IIII). A third is mixing the order, since numerals are generally written from largest value on the left to smallest on the right, apart from the deliberate subtractive pairs.

It is worth knowing that these rules are modern conventions as much as ancient law. The Romans themselves were less strict, you will still see IIII rather than IV on many traditional clock faces, a stylistic choice that persists precisely because it looks balanced against the VIII opposite it. For most purposes, though, the tidy modern rules are what people expect, and following them makes your numerals unambiguous. When you need to write or read a specific value, a copyright year, a chapter number, a monarch's ordinal, the safest approach is to convert it with a tool that applies the standard rules, then trust that result. The subtraction rule is where nearly all confusion lives, so once you have internalised the valid subtractive pairs (IV, IX, XL, XC, CD, CM) you have mastered the part that actually trips people up.

In summary, Roman numerals are built from seven letters that you mostly add from largest to smallest, with the subtraction rule handling the numbers just below five and ten to avoid repeated symbols. There is no zero, which is why they are poor for arithmetic but perfectly good for the formal, decorative labelling where they still survive. Learn the seven symbols and the subtraction pattern, and those letters on clocks and cornerstones become plain numbers you can read at a glance.

How Emoji Actually Work (and How to Use Them Well)

Quick answer

Why emoji look different across devices, how they are really just characters, and how to use them to add clarity and tone.

By 123MiniApps · Published 2026-07-31 · Updated 2026-09-01 · 1161 words · about 5 minute read

Emoji feel like little pictures, but underneath they are just characters, the same kind of thing as letters and digits, defined by the Unicode standard that governs all text on computers. That single fact explains most of what is puzzling about them: why the same emoji looks different on different phones, why some count as several characters, and why new ones appear every year. The Emoji Picker lets you find and copy any emoji in your browser, and this article explains how they really work and how to use them well.

Understanding that emoji are text, not images, demystifies their quirks and helps you use them more effectively, as a genuine layer of tone and clarity in writing, rather than random decoration.

Emoji are characters, not pictures

When you send an emoji, you are not sending an image; you are sending a code, a number defined by Unicode, exactly as the letter 'A' is the number 65. The receiving device looks up that code and draws its own version of the emoji. This is why emoji can appear in text fields, be counted, searched and copied like any other character: to the computer, they are characters. The picture you see is just the current device's rendering of the underlying code, in the same way a letter is drawn in whatever font your device uses. This character-based nature is the key to everything else about how emoji behave.

Why the same emoji looks different everywhere

Because an emoji is a code rather than a picture, each platform draws its own artwork for it. Unicode defines what an emoji is, 'grinning face', 'red heart', but not exactly how it should look, so the phone maker, operating system or app designs its own version. The result is that the 'same' emoji can look meaningfully different across devices: friendlier on one, more neutral on another, occasionally different enough to change the perceived tone. This has caused genuine miscommunication when an emoji that looks cheerful on the sender's phone appears flat or odd on the recipient's. It is worth remembering that the person you are messaging may not be seeing quite the same face you sent.

One emoji can be several characters

Some emoji are built by combining codes, a family emoji, or a skin-tone variant, is several underlying characters joined together. That is why a single emoji can count as multiple characters against a length limit, and why deleting one sometimes takes more than one press.

Why emoji keep multiplying

New emoji appear regularly because Unicode adds them in periodic updates, responding to what people want to express, new foods, animals, activities, symbols, and more inclusive options like varied skin tones and gender representations. Each addition is a new code that devices then design artwork for, which is why a brand-new emoji sometimes shows as a blank box on an older device that has not yet learned that code. The steady expansion reflects emoji doing what language always does: growing to cover the things people need to say. A good picker keeps up with the current set so you can find whatever has been added.

Using emoji well in writing

Emoji are not just fun; used deliberately they add real value to writing:

  • Tone: a single emoji can signal that a message is friendly or joking, heading off the flatness that makes plain text easy to misread.
  • Clarity: an emoji can mark a point, a warning or a positive note at a glance.
  • Scannability: a leading emoji helps items in a list stand apart.
  • Warmth: a well-placed emoji makes a message feel human rather than curt.

The key is restraint and context. A couple of well-chosen emoji clarify and warm a message; a dozen scattered through it become noise. And register matters, playful in a chat with friends, sparing or none in a formal document. Matching emoji use to the setting is what separates effective use from clutter.

Emoji among the character tools

Because emoji are characters, they connect to the other tools that deal with text at the code level. A text to ASCII tool reveals the numeric codes behind characters, which is exactly what emoji are under the surface; a character counter shows why an emoji-laden message can hit a length limit faster than expected; and a meme generator puts emoji and text together for visual jokes. Seeing emoji as part of the text system, rather than as separate images, ties all of this together.

Emoji, accessibility and search

Two practical details are worth knowing because they affect how emoji are received beyond how they look. The first is accessibility: screen readers, which read text aloud for people with visual impairments, announce emoji by their official Unicode names. A 'face with tears of joy' is read out as exactly that. This means a message packed with emoji can become a tiring stream of spoken descriptions for someone using a screen reader, and an emoji used in place of a word forces the listener to infer meaning from its name. Using emoji in moderation, and not relying on them to carry essential meaning, keeps your writing accessible to everyone.

The second is that emoji carry meaning that can shift with context and over time, sometimes in ways the sender does not intend. Because they are a shared visual vocabulary, particular emoji acquire connotations, some playful, some ironic, some that differ sharply between generations or communities, and an emoji meant innocently can read very differently to another audience. The safest approach in anything that matters is to use emoji whose common meaning is clear and uncontroversial, and to lean on them for tone and emphasis rather than as coded messages open to misreading. Combined with the earlier point that the recipient may see different artwork than you sent, this is a reminder that emoji, for all their friendliness, are a form of communication that rewards a little care. Understanding that they are shared characters with device-specific looks and culturally-shaped meanings, not private pictures, is what lets you use them to add genuine warmth and clarity rather than accidental confusion, and a good picker simply makes finding the right one quick so you can focus on using it well.

To recap: emoji are Unicode characters, not pictures, which is why they behave like text, countable, copyable, searchable, and why each device draws its own version, so the same emoji can look different to your recipient. Some are built from several combined codes, hence the odd character counts, and new ones arrive with each Unicode update. Used with restraint and an eye to context, emoji add tone, clarity and warmth that plain text lacks. Understand that they are characters at heart, pick them from a good picker, and use a few well rather than many carelessly, and emoji become a genuinely useful part of how you write.
